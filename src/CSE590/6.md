# Lecture 6: Operational Semantics and Inductive Definitions

**Main ideas:** inference rules define evaluation; finite derivations support induction proofs.

## Reading inference rules

$$
\frac{J_1\qquad\cdots\qquad J_k}{J}
\quad(\text{Rule name}).
$$

| Part | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Judgment $J$ | Claim of a specified form, e.g. $e\to e'$ |
| Premises, above line | Claims needed to apply the rule |
| Conclusion, below line | Claim established by the rule |
| Axiom | Rule with no premises |
| Side condition | Extra restriction, e.g. $x\ne y$ |
| Rule instance | Metavariables consistently replaced by concrete terms |

To prove a judgment, match it to a rule's conclusion, then prove every premise. Continue until reaching axioms. A derivation records these justifications; each node must match an actual rule.

Terms $e::=x\mid\lambda x.e\mid e_1\,e_2$; values $v::=\lambda x.e$.

- Lambda body need not be normalized.
- Usually evaluate **closed** terms.
- Open terms can be stuck: $x\,v$ is neither a value nor able to step.

## Call-by-value (CBV)

Order: **function → argument → substitute**. Capture-avoiding substitution throughout.

$$
\frac{}{(\lambda x.e)\,v\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}e[x\mapsto v]}
\quad(\text{Beta})
$$

$$
\frac{e_1\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}e_1'}
{e_1\,e_2\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}e_1'\,e_2}
\quad(\text{AppL})
\qquad
\frac{e_2\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}e_2'}
{v_1\,e_2\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}v_1\,e_2'}
\quad(\text{AppR})
$$

No rule under lambdas. AppR requires the function to be a value; Beta requires the argument to be a value.

The rules define the evaluation order through their restrictions. AppL works while the function can step; AppR steps the argument only after the function is a value; Beta applies once the argument is also a value. With no rule for entering a lambda, evaluating a function does not execute its body.

**Sequence versus proof tree.** Let $I=\lambda z.z$:

$$
(\lambda f.f)\,(I\,I)
\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}(\lambda f.f)\,I
\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}I.
$$

Proof of the **first step only**:

$$
\frac{
 \displaystyle\frac{}{I\,I\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}I}\;(\text{Beta})
}{
 (\lambda f.f)\,(I\,I)\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}(\lambda f.f)\,I
}\quad(\text{AppR}).
$$

Read from the leaves: Beta proves the argument step $I\,I\to I$; AppR lifts that step into the whole application because $\lambda f.f$ is already a value. Only the argument changes during this whole-program step.

## Call-by-name (CBN)

Order: **function → substitute unevaluated argument**.

$$
\frac{}{(\lambda x.e_1)\,e_2\to_{\mathrm{cbn}}e_1[x\mapsto e_2]}
\quad(\text{Beta})
$$

$$
\frac{e_1\to_{\mathrm{cbn}}e_1'}
{e_1\,e_2\to_{\mathrm{cbn}}e_1'\,e_2}
\quad(\text{AppL})
$$

No AppR; no reduction under lambdas.

$$
(\lambda x.I)\,\Omega\to_{\mathrm{cbn}}I.
$$

CBV loops on the same input by repeatedly stepping its argument.

**Both strategies:**

- $e\to^*e'$: zero or more steps.
- Termination: reach a value. Divergence: infinitely many steps.
- Deterministic up to alpha equivalence: at most one next result.
- Determinism proof: rule cases disjoint because values cannot step; induction gives uniqueness of subterm steps.

Stopping and succeeding are different notions. A value is a designated successful result; an open term such as $x\,v$ is stuck because no rule applies. Divergence is different again: steps remain possible forever. Determinism alone does not tell us which outcome occurs.

## Delaying CBV computation

Ordinary $\mathsf{If}\;b\;e_t\;e_f$ evaluates both branch arguments. Thus $\mathsf{If}\;\mathsf{True}\;I\;\Omega$ diverges.

**Thunk:** $\lambda u.e$, with fresh $u$; already a value. Apply to dummy value $I$ to run $e$.

Using the ordinary Church booleans from [Lecture 4](4.md):

$$
\mathsf{If}_{\mathrm{cbv}}
\triangleq\lambda b.\lambda t.\lambda f.(b\,t\,f)\,I.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{If}_{\mathrm{cbv}}\;\mathsf{True}\;(\lambda u.e_t)\;(\lambda u.e_f)
&\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}^*(\lambda u.e_t)\,I\\
&\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}e_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both thunks are values; only the chosen body runs.

For example, choosing between $\lambda u.I$ and $\lambda u.\Omega$ can return $I$ without evaluating $\Omega$. The dummy argument's value is irrelevant because fresh $u$ never occurs in the selected body.

**Alternative from the board:** build forcing into the booleans.

$$
\mathsf{True}_{\mathrm{cbv}}=\lambda t.\lambda f.t\,I,
\qquad
\mathsf{False}_{\mathrm{cbv}}=\lambda t.\lambda f.f\,I.
$$

Then use $b\,(\lambda u.e_t)\,(\lambda u.e_f)$ directly. Boolean producers such as $\mathsf{IsZero}$ must also return the new booleans. Do not combine this convention with $\mathsf{If}_{\mathrm{cbv}}$: that would force twice.

### CBV factorial

Use $Y_{\mathrm{cbv}}$ from [Lecture 5](5.md), ordinary Church booleans, and branch thunks:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{\mathrm{cbv}}\triangleq\lambda r.\lambda n.
\mathsf{If}_{\mathrm{cbv}}\;&(\mathsf{IsZero}\;n)\\
& (\lambda u.\overline1)\\
& (\lambda u.\mathsf{Mul}\;n\;(r\;(\mathsf{Pred}\;n))).
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\mathsf{Fact}_{\mathrm{cbv}}\triangleq Y_{\mathrm{cbv}}\;T_{\mathrm{cbv}}.
$$

Two necessary delays:

1. $Y_{\mathrm{cbv}}$: delay recursive unfolding until a call.
2. Branch thunks: avoid a recursive call in the zero case.

On numeral inputs: returns a function representing $n!$. Weak CBV need not normalize its body to the written numeral $\overline{n!}$.

At input zero, the true branch thunk is selected and forced, returning $\overline1$. The recursive call remains inside the discarded false thunk, so it never starts. At a positive input, forcing the false thunk makes one recursive call on the predecessor and multiplies its result by the current numeral.

## Inductive definitions and well-foundedness

**Inductive definition:** least set closed under the rules; exactly the judgments with finite proof trees.

$$
\frac{}{0\;\mathsf{isNat}},
\qquad
\frac{n\;\mathsf{isNat}}{(n+1)\;\mathsf{isNat}}.
$$

Zero starts a proof; Successor builds larger proofs. A cyclic rule $J/J$ alone proves nothing.

“Least” excludes unsupported judgments: begin with axioms and add only conclusions whose premises are already justified. A larger set closed under those rules might contain extra facts; taking the least one excludes them.

**Well-founded relation $\prec$:** no infinite descending chain. Induction principle:

$$
\Bigl[\forall a.\;
 (\forall b\prec a.\;P(b))\Rightarrow P(a)\Bigr]
\Longrightarrow\forall a.\;P(a).
$$

| Relation | Well-founded? |
| --- | --- |
| $<$ on $\mathbb N$ | Yes: cannot decrease forever |
| $<$ on $\mathbb Z$ | No: $0,-1,-2,\ldots$ |
| Proper subterm of finite syntax | Yes: fewer nodes |
| Beta reduction | No: $\Omega\to_\beta\Omega\to_\beta\cdots$ |

Well-foundedness ensures that repeatedly reducing a proof obligation to smaller objects eventually reaches a case with no smaller objects. Structural induction uses syntax size for this purpose; rule induction uses derivation size. Neither method requires the program being studied to terminate.

**Choose the induction object carefully:**

**IH = induction hypothesis:** assume the desired property for smaller objects, then prove it for the current one. State the property and what gets smaller before starting the cases.

| Method | Cases / hypotheses |
| --- | --- |
| Structural induction on $e$ | Variable; abstraction with body IH; application with two subterm IHs |
| Rule induction on a derivation | One case per final rule; IH for each premise derivation |
| Induction on a finite run | Zero steps; one step followed by a shorter run |

A reduct may be larger than its source because substitution duplicates terms. Infinite evaluation does not invalidate induction on a **finite derivation** of one step.

## Proof 1: substitution bounds free variables

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{FV}(e[x\mapsto s])
\subseteq(\mathrm{FV}(e)\setminus\{x\})\cup\mathrm{FV}(s).}
$$

Induct on term size; keep $x,s$ arbitrary. Same constructor cases as structural induction, with fresh renaming justified by unchanged size.

- **Variable $y$:** $y=x$ gives $s$; otherwise gives $y$.
- **Application:** apply both IHs; take the union.
- **Abstraction $\lambda y.e_0$: $y=x$:** substitution stops; bound follows directly.
- **Abstraction: $y\ne x$:** alpha-rename so $y\notin\mathrm{FV}(s)$; apply IH to the smaller body.

Last case:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{FV}((\lambda y.e_0)[x\mapsto s])
&=\mathrm{FV}(e_0[x\mapsto s])\setminus\{y\}\\
&\subseteq
 \bigl((\mathrm{FV}(e_0)\setminus\{x\})\cup\mathrm{FV}(s)\bigr)
 \setminus\{y\}\\
&=(\mathrm{FV}(\lambda y.e_0)\setminus\{x\})\cup\mathrm{FV}(s).
\end{aligned}
$$

Last equality uses freshness of $y$; alpha renaming preserves free variables.

In that calculation, the first line unfolds substitution and free variables; the second applies the IH to the body; the last rearranges sets. Since $y$ is not free in $s$, removing $y$ cannot remove anything contributed by $s$. This is the precise point where freshness is needed.

## Proof 2: evaluation introduces no new free variables

$$
e\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}e'
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{FV}(e')\subseteq\mathrm{FV}(e).
$$

The two proofs fit together: substitution can insert free names from the argument, but that argument was already part of the original application. Beta reduction therefore introduces no name absent from the whole source term.

**Rule induction:** three possible final rules.

| Case | Proof step |
| --- | --- |
| Beta | Substitution lemma; no premise IH |
| AppL | IH: $\mathrm{FV}(e_1')\subseteq\mathrm{FV}(e_1)$; union with $\mathrm{FV}(e_2)$ |
| AppR | IH: $\mathrm{FV}(e_2')\subseteq\mathrm{FV}(e_2)$; union with $\mathrm{FV}(v_1)$ |

Beta case explicitly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{FV}(e_0[x\mapsto v])
&\subseteq(\mathrm{FV}(e_0)\setminus\{x\})\cup\mathrm{FV}(v)\\
&=\mathrm{FV}((\lambda x.e_0)\,v).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Consequences:** same property for CBN and finite runs; closedness preserved. Inclusion may be strict:

$$
(\lambda x.I)\,(\lambda z.y)\to_{\mathrm{cbv}}I
\qquad(y\ne z),
$$

discarding free $y$.
