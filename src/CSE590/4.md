# Lecture 4: Reduction, Confluence, and Church Encodings

**Main ideas:** reduction choices can rejoin; functions can encode data. Throughout: capture-avoiding substitution, terms identified up to alpha equivalence.

## Reduction and normal forms

**Beta:** apply a function by substitution.

$$
(\lambda x.e)\,s\to_\beta e[x\mapsto s].
$$

**Full beta reduction:** allowed anywhere, including inside lambda bodies.

$$
\frac{e_1\to_\beta e_1'}{e_1\,e_2\to_\beta e_1'\,e_2},
\qquad
\frac{e_2\to_\beta e_2'}{e_1\,e_2\to_\beta e_1\,e_2'},
\qquad
\frac{e\to_\beta e'}{\lambda x.e\to_\beta\lambda x.e'}.
$$

Each fraction reads “if the step above is possible, the step below is allowed.” These **context rules** let one subterm change while its surroundings stay fixed. Several rules may offer different next steps.

| Notation / term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $e\to_\beta^*e'$ | Zero or more forward steps |
| $e=_\beta e'$ | Beta convertibility: forward **or backward** steps, allowing alpha renaming |
| Beta normal form | No beta redex anywhere; e.g. $\lambda x.x$ or $x\,y$ |

An application is itself a beta redex only when its function part is a lambda; its subterms may still contain other redexes. In $x\,y$, neither the whole application nor either variable can reduce, so it is a normal form. By contrast, $\lambda x.(\lambda y.y)\,x$ is not: full reduction can simplify its body to obtain $\lambda x.x$.

**Eta:** remove a wrapper that only forwards its argument.

$$
\lambda x.e\,x\to_\eta e
\qquad x\notin\mathrm{FV}(e).
$$

- Valid: $\lambda f.\lambda y.f\,y\to_\eta\lambda f.f$.
- Invalid: $\lambda x.x\,x\to_\eta x$; side condition fails.
- CBV caution: $\lambda x.\Omega\,x$ is a value; $\Omega$ diverges. Unrestricted eta contraction can change termination.

The freshness condition means $e$ does not depend on the wrapper's parameter. For a fresh argument $a$, both the wrapper and $e$ produce $e\,a$. Without that condition, removing the binder can expose a previously bound variable.

## Divergence versus normalization

$$
\omega\triangleq\lambda x.x\,x,
\qquad
\Omega\triangleq\omega\,\omega\to_\beta\omega\,\omega=\Omega.
$$

- $\omega$: normal form.
- $\Omega$: only redex reproduces itself; no normal form.
- **Weakly normalizing:** some reduction sequence reaches a normal form.
- **Strongly normalizing:** every reduction sequence terminates.

In $\omega$, the body $x\,x$ has a variable in function position, so it contains no beta redex. Applying $\omega$ to itself replaces both occurrences of $x$ by $\omega$, recreating $\Omega$. The infinite behavior comes from repeating a finite substitution step, not from an infinitely large starting term.

With $I=\lambda y.y$:

$$
(\lambda x.I)\,\Omega\to_\beta I.
$$

Weakly normalizing, but not strongly: repeatedly reducing the argument loops forever.

## Confluence: Church–Rosser

Any two finite reduction paths from the same term can rejoin:

Think of reductions as paths through a graph of terms. Confluence says that choosing different branches cannot lead to incompatible normal forms. The joining term $q$ need not itself be normal; the theorem concerns agreement between paths, even when further computation remains.

$$
\bigl(e\to_\beta^*e_1\land e\to_\beta^*e_2\bigr)
\Longrightarrow
\exists q.\;e_1\to_\beta^*q\land e_2\to_\beta^*q.
$$

Example: let $a=(\lambda y.y)\,z$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\lambda x.x\,x)\,a
&\to_\beta(\lambda x.x\,x)\,z\to_\beta z\,z,
\\
(\lambda x.x\,x)\,a
&\to_\beta a\,a\to_\beta z\,a\to_\beta z\,z.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Consequence:** normal forms unique up to alpha equivalence.

Proof outline: two normal forms must have a common reduct; neither can step, so they must already agree.

**Confluence versus determinism:** confluence allows several next steps that later rejoin. Determinism permits at most one next step. Full beta reduction is confluent but not deterministic.

**Limits:** no termination guarantee; joining may require multiple steps. Strategy choice still matters ([Lecture 5](5.md)).

## Church encodings

**Data as functions:** booleans select; numerals iterate; pairs supply components to a consumer. Names below abbreviate lambda terms; equations use full beta reduction.

An encoding implements data through the operations that use it. There is no primitive Boolean or pair hidden underneath: expanding every abbreviation leaves only variables, lambdas, and applications. The notation $\triangleq$ means “defined to be.”

### Booleans

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{True}&\triangleq\lambda t.\lambda f.t,\\
\mathsf{False}&\triangleq\lambda t.\lambda f.f,\\
\mathsf{If}&\triangleq\lambda b.\lambda t.\lambda f.b\,t\,f,\\
\mathsf{Not}&\triangleq\lambda b.b\;\mathsf{False}\;\mathsf{True},\\
\mathsf{And}&\triangleq\lambda p.\lambda q.p\,q\,\mathsf{False},\\
\mathsf{Or}&\triangleq\lambda p.\lambda q.p\,\mathsf{True}\,q.
\end{aligned}
$$

| Application | Result |
| --- | --- |
| $\mathsf{If}\;\mathsf{True}\;t\;f$ | $t$ |
| $\mathsf{If}\;\mathsf{False}\;t\;f$ | $f$ |
| $\mathsf{And}\;p\;q$ | If $p$, choose $q$; otherwise false |
| $\mathsf{Or}\;p\;q$ | If $p$, choose true; otherwise $q$ |

CBV needs delayed branches; see [Lecture 6](6.md).

To read an encoding, ask what arguments it expects. $\mathsf{True}$ expects two choices and returns the first; $\mathsf{Not}$ reverses those choices by supplying false first and true second.

### Numerals: repeat an operation

$$
\overline n\triangleq\lambda f.\lambda x.f^n(x),
\qquad f^0(x)=x,\quad f^{n+1}(x)=f(f^n(x)).
$$

$$
\overline0=\lambda f.\lambda x.x,
\qquad
\overline1=\lambda f.\lambda x.f\,x,
\qquad
\overline2=\lambda f.\lambda x.f\,(f\,x).
$$

$\overline n\,f\,x$: start at $x$, apply $f$ exactly $n$ times. Bar = encoded number; $f^n$ = repeated application.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{Succ}&\triangleq\lambda n.\lambda f.\lambda x.f\,(n\,f\,x),\\
\mathsf{Add}&\triangleq\lambda n.\lambda m.n\;\mathsf{Succ}\;m,\\
\mathsf{Mul}&\triangleq\lambda n.\lambda m.n\;(\mathsf{Add}\;m)\;\overline0,\\
\mathsf{Exp}&\triangleq\lambda n.\lambda m.n\;(\mathsf{Mul}\;m)\;\overline1.
\end{aligned}
$$

| Operation | Iteration | Result on encoded inputs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathsf{Succ}\;\overline n$ | One extra application of $f$ | $\overline{n+1}$ |
| $\mathsf{Add}\;\overline n\;\overline m$ | Increment $m$, $n$ times | $\overline{n+m}$ |
| $\mathsf{Mul}\;\overline n\;\overline m$ | Add $m$ to $0$, $n$ times | $\overline{nm}$ |
| $\mathsf{Exp}\;\overline n\;\overline m$ | Multiply $1$ by $m$, $n$ times | $\overline{m^n}$ |

**Exponent first:** $\mathsf{Exp}\;\overline2\;\overline3\to_\beta^*\overline9$. Zero repetitions return $\overline1$, including the $0^0$ case under this encoding.

Example: $\mathsf{Add}\;\overline2\;\overline3$ becomes $\overline2\;\mathsf{Succ}\;\overline3$, then $\mathsf{Succ}\;(\mathsf{Succ}\;\overline3)$, giving $\overline5$. Multiplication repeats this addition idea; exponentiation repeats multiplication.

### Pairs and local bindings

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{Pair}&\triangleq\lambda a.\lambda b.\lambda k.k\,a\,b,\\
\mathsf{First}&\triangleq\lambda p.p\;\mathsf{True},\\
\mathsf{Second}&\triangleq\lambda p.p\;\mathsf{False}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\mathsf{First}\;(\mathsf{Pair}\;a\;b)
\to_\beta^*\mathsf{True}\;a\;b\to_\beta^*a.
$$

The pair stores its components in $\lambda k.k\,a\,b$. A projection supplies the consumer $k$: $\mathsf{True}$ selects $a$, $\mathsf{False}$ selects $b$.

**Syntactic sugar:** new notation, translated into existing terms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{let}\;x=e_1\;\mathsf{in}\;e_2
&\triangleq(\lambda x.e_2)\,e_1,\\
\mathsf{let}\;(x,y)=p\;\mathsf{in}\;e
&\triangleq p\;(\lambda x.\lambda y.e).
\end{aligned}
$$

- Ordinary `let`: $x$ bound only in $e_2$; nonrecursive.
- Pair binding: distinct $x,y$; consumer receives components in order.

Desugaring lets us explain a convenient language feature using rules we already have. In `let x = e1 in e2`, the lambda creates the scope of $x$ and application supplies its value. A recursive binding would need an additional mechanism because $x$ is not bound inside $e_1$.
